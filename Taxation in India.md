# Different Types of Taxes

- **Difference Between Tax, Cess, and Surcharge** (15:49-20:08): The lecture clarifies the distinctions:
    - **Cess:** A tax on tax, levied for a specific purpose (e.g., education cess, Swachh Bharat cess), with the collected money being used solely for that purpose.
    - **Surcharge:** An additional tax on tax, applied to high-income earners without any specific allocation of funds. Unlike regular taxes, cess and surcharge are not shared between the central and state governments.
- **Classification of Taxes** (20:09-29:32): Taxes are classified based on their impact and incidence:
    - **Direct Tax:** Where the impact (who is initially taxed) and incidence (who ultimately bears the burden) fall on the same person (e.g., income tax, wealth tax).
    - **Indirect Tax:** Where the impact and incidence fall on different people (e.g., GST on goods and services, where the seller is taxed but the burden is passed to the consumer).
    - **Progressive Tax:** Tax rate increases with income (e.g., income tax).
    - **Regressive Tax:** Tax rate decreases as income increases, disproportionately affecting lower-income groups (e.g., indirect taxes like GST).
    - **Proportional Tax:** A flat tax rate regardless of income or value (e.g., a fixed percentage tax on goods).
- **Types of Taxes** (29:33-43:12): The video lists various types of direct and indirect taxes:
    - **Direct Taxes** (29:39 - 37:40)
		- **Income Tax**: This is a tax paid by **individuals** on their income (29:46 - 29:55).
		- **Corporate Tax**: This is the tax paid by **business houses** on the profits they earn (29:57 - 30:20).
		- **Minimum Alternate Tax (MAT)**: This is a minimum tax applied to companies that claim exemptions and otherwise wouldn't pay corporate tax. Companies must pay a proportion, specifically **18.5%** of their book profit (30:57 - 32:16).
		- **Dividend Distribution Tax (DDT)**: This was a tax paid by companies on the dividends distributed to their shareholders. The video states that it was **abolished in 2020** (32:21 - 33:06).
		- **Securities Transaction Tax (STT)**: This tax is levied when individuals buy or sell **securities** (stocks) in the market (33:10 - 33:40).
		- **Wealth Tax**: This tax was applied to an individual's total accumulated wealth, including bungalows, cars, and land. The video mentions that the government **abolished it in 2016** (33:51 - 34:11).
		- **Capital Gain Tax**: This tax is applied to the **profit** made from selling an asset. It is further categorized into:
		    - **Short-Term Capital Gain Tax (STCG)**: Applies if an asset (excluding securities) is held for **less than 36 months** (35:10 - 35:50), or if securities are held for **less than 12 months** (36:45 - 37:05).
		    - **Long-Term Capital Gain Tax (LTCG)**: Applies if an asset (excluding securities) is held for **more than 36 months** (35:51 - 36:06), or if securities are held for **more than 12 months** (37:08 - 37:18).
	- **Indirect Taxes** (37:41 - 43:12)
		- **Excise Duty**: This was a tax applied to **manufacturing activities** within the country. The video states it has been **replaced by Central GST** (37:50 - 38:15).
		- **Custom Duty**: This tax is levied on **imports and exports** (38:19 - 38:25). It includes:
		- **Safeguard Duty**: Applied to prevent excessive exports of certain commodities (like wheat) to protect domestic food security (38:28 - 39:28).
		- **Countervailing Duty**: Applied to counteract subsidies provided by other countries to their producers, making their imported goods cheaper (39:31 - 41:18).
		- **Anti-Dumping Duty**: Imposed when a country is "dumping" goods into another market by selling them at a price below their cost of production to capture market share (41:19 - 43:12).
		- **Goods and Services Tax (GST)**: (Lecture 14 from hereon)
			- **Central GST (CGST)** and **State GST (SGST)**: These are applied when an **intra-state sale** occurs, meaning the product is manufactured and sold within the same state (9:06). For example, if a bicycle is made and sold in Gujarat, both CGST and SGST would be levied. The CGST portion would go to the Central Government, and the SGST portion would go to the State Government (9:09 - 9:35).
			- **Integrated GST (IGST)**: This is applied when an **inter-state sale** occurs, meaning the product moves from one state to another (9:37). For instance, if a bicycle manufactured in Gujarat is sold to Rajasthan, IGST would be applied to that transaction (9:42 - 9:49).

# Reasons Behind India's Low Tax-to-GDP Ratio

The video lists several factors contributing to India's low tax to GDP ratio:

- **Dominance of the Agriculture Sector:** A large portion of the population works in the agricultural sector, and income derived from agriculture is exempt from income tax under Section 10 of the Income Tax Act. This significantly reduces the potential tax base for the government (25:12 - 25:55).
- **Large Presence of the Informal Sector:** Approximately 93% of the population works in the informal sector, where tax deduction at source (TDS) is not applicable. This makes tax collection difficult for the government, hindering revenue generation (25:58 - 26:54).
- **Low Per Capita Income:** Many people in India do not fall into the tax bracket due to low per capita income, which limits the number of individuals liable to pay income tax (26:56 - 27:05).
- **Tax Exemptions:** The government has introduced various tax exemptions, such as reductions in corporate tax and dividend distribution tax, and tax holidays for startups (e.g., no tax for the first five years). These measures, while aimed at economic revival and self-reliance, reduce the government's tax revenue (27:08 - 27:40).
- **Unresolved Tax Disputes:** A significant number of tax disputes remain unresolved for extended periods due to lengthy court proceedings. While a case is pending, the tax liability is not collected, contributing to lower tax collection (27:43 - 28:32).

# How GST Removes Cascading Effect?

The video explains that **GST eliminates the cascading effect** primarily through the mechanism of **Input Tax Credit (ITC)** (17:07 - 17:11).

Before GST, taxes were applied on values that already included previously paid taxes, leading to a "tax on tax" situation (10:35 - 10:50). For example, a manufacturer would pay excise duty, and then a dealer would pay VAT on the manufacturer's price, which already included the excise duty. This meant the VAT was applied not just on the value added by the manufacturer, but also on the tax already paid (12:08 - 12:44).

With GST, when a dealer purchases goods from a manufacturer and pays tax, this tax becomes an "Input Tax Credit" for the dealer (15:50 - 15:58). When the dealer then sells the goods to the end customer and collects GST, the dealer can offset the tax they already paid to the manufacturer against the tax they collected from the customer. This ensures that the tax is only paid on the **value added** at each stage of the supply chain, rather than on the gross value that includes previous taxes (17:58 - 18:05).

The example provided illustrates this:

- A car manufactured for ₹5 lakh (11:18 - 11:23).
- Pre-GST, excise duty would be added, then VAT would be applied on the cost including excise duty, leading to tax on tax (12:08 - 12:44).
- With GST, if a manufacturer sells a car to a dealer for ₹5 lakh plus ₹1 lakh in GST (assuming 10% each for CGST and SGST on ₹5 lakh, making ₹50,000 each, total ₹1 lakh), the dealer pays ₹6 lakh (14:51 - 15:02).
- When the dealer adds a profit margin (e.g., ₹50,000) and sells the car to the end customer for ₹5.50 lakh, the GST is calculated on this ₹5.50 lakh (16:40 - 16:53).
- The dealer collects ₹1.10 lakh in GST (assuming 10% each, total 20% on ₹5.50 lakh). Since the dealer already paid ₹1 lakh in GST to the manufacturer, they only need to deposit the difference of ₹10,000 to the government. This ₹10,000 represents the tax on the ₹50,000 value added by the dealer (17:48 - 18:25).

This system ensures that tax is only levied on the **value addition** at each stage of production and supply, effectively removing the cascading effect of taxes (17:58 - 18:01).

# Crowding Out Effect

The "crowding out effect" occurs when the government borrows a large portion of the money available in the market (22:34). This leaves less money for the private sector to borrow and invest, which can hinder the establishment of new businesses or the expansion of existing ones (23:09-23:18).
