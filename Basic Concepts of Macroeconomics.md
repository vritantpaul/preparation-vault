What are <b>current transfers</b>?
Back: Payments made out of <i>current income</i>* and paid to support <i>current consumption</i>. E.g. old age pension, gifts, unemployment allowances, etc.
Footnotes: "Made out of current income" means that payment is financed from the **regular, recurring income earned during the current period**, not from selling assets or using accumulated wealth.  
E.g. A government pays old-age pension from its annual tax revenue → current income → current transfer.

What are <b>capital goods</b>?
Back: Goods purchased by <i>firms</i> for <i>capital formation</i> or <i>investment</i>. E.g. machinery purchased by a firm.
Footnotes: Capital goods have similar attributes as intermediate goods.  
1. For e.g., they are used for the process of production, but unlike, single-use producer goods, they don't lose their identity in the production process.  
2. Also, like intermediate goods they have derived demand.  

→ One more thing to note about capital goods is that they depreciate over a period of time.

What is the difference between direct and indirect tax?
Back: Direct tax has the <i>same</i> impact and incidence*, whereas, indirect tax has <i>different</i> impact and incidence.
Footnotes: Tax <b>impact</b> is the <u>initial point where a tax is levied</u> (e.g., on a manufacturer), while tax <b>incidence</b> is the <u>final resting place of the financial burden</u> (e.g., the consumer who pays higher prices) after potential tax shifting.

What are the characteristics of positive economics*?
Back: 
1. Descriptive
2. Based on data and cause-effect
3. No value judgments

Footnotes: <u>Examples</u>:
- “An increase in price reduces quantity demanded.”
- “India’s GDP grew by 7%.”
- “Higher taxes may reduce consumption.”
