# The Environmental and Societal Impact of AI Data Centers: Navigating the Complex Costs of an Emerging Technology

## Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (*AI*) is rapidly transforming industries and society, evoking a spectrum of opinions—inspiring optimism and fear alike. At the heart of its expansion lie **hyperscale AI data centers**, massive industrial facilities dedicated exclusively to powering AI workloads. This chapter explores the emerging realities surrounding these data centers, focusing on their **energy consumption**, **water usage**, **carbon footprint**, and **local community impacts**. The significance of these issues lies not only in their environmental magnitude but also in the opaque nature of the data and decisions shaping AI’s footprint. Understanding key concepts such as **Koomey’s Law** (which describes the doubling of compute efficiency every 1.6 years), the **Jevons Paradox** (where greater efficiency paradoxically drives greater overall consumption), and **scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions** (different categories of greenhouse gas emissions related to operations and supply chains) is critical to grasping the multi-dimensional challenges AI poses today.

## The Scale and Energy Demands of AI Data Centers

Hyperscale AI data centers are characterized by enormous physical size and compute power, employing tens of thousands of GPU chips in racks consuming vastly more electricity than conventional server racks.

- A single rack of AI GPUs can consume 5 to 30 times the power of a typical server rack—comparable in size to a refrigerator but using as much electricity as 65 homes.
- To qualify as "hyperscale," a US data center must draw more than 100 megawatts, equivalent to the power consumption of 80,000 homes.
- From 2014 to 2024, electricity use by data centers in the US tripled, reaching an estimated 4.7% of total US electricity consumption by 2024—roughly one-seventh of the electricity used by all US homes.
- This growth is geographically uneven; while some local grids can accommodate these facilities without strain, others face stress due to inadequate infrastructure.

Despite these large figures, early fears paralleling those from the 1990s internet boom—predictions that computers would drain electricity resources catastrophically—have proven exaggerated. Improvements in energy efficiency, as codified in **Koomey’s Law**, historically kept power consumption from exploding. However, AI is shifting this balance, introducing a fresh "electricity vampire" that may disrupt past trends.

- AI data centers are rapidly outpacing classical improvements in energy efficiency.
- Hyperscale centers are proliferating: currently, 68 are built or under construction in the US, with 267 more approved or planned.
- Projections suggest data center electricity use could comprise 7-12% of US demand by 2028, but uncertainty remains high.

## Water Usage and Cooling Challenges

Water, a finite and location-specific resource, is consumed heavily by AI data centers primarily to cool their servers and to generate electricity indirectly.

- US data centers directly consumed about 17 billion gallons of water in 2023, primarily for cooling; when including water used in power generation, this jumps to 228 billion gallons—approximately two-thirds of New York City’s yearly drinking water consumption.
- Future estimates warn usage could surge to over 840 billion gallons annually, equivalent to the combined needs of New York City and Chicago.
- Hyperscale data centers like one in Abilene, Texas use **closed loop cooling systems**, which recirculate water to limit loss, requiring an upfront fill of millions of gallons. Despite water-saving designs, the initial volume and local infrastructure capacity are concerns.
- Cooling methods involve a trade-off between **evaporative cooling** (more water-efficient but water-consuming, especially problematic in hot seasons) and **radiative cooling** (less water use but higher electricity demand for fans).
- Water availability is a **local issue**: for example, in Phoenix, Arizona, water use for AI cooling might increase nearly tenfold from 385 million to 3.7 billion gallons annually amid a regional drought intensified by Colorado River declines.

This growing local water demand represents a critical bottleneck, as water cannot be created or easily relocated across regions. Data center expansion thus risks compounding water stress, especially in already vulnerable areas.

## Power Generation Strategies and Environmental Implications

To meet massive power needs without relying solely on sometimes inadequate local grids, many data center operators are investing in **behind-the-meter generation**—owning and operating onsite power plants, predominantly fueled by **natural gas turbines**.

- Approximately 25% of planned data center expansions involve building private power plants; around 2-3% of these plants have been constructed, but momentum is shifting quickly.
- Notably, the "GW Ranch" data center in West Texas plans a 7-gigawatt gas turbine complex, potentially the largest fossil fuel power plant in the US, consuming over 1% of the nation’s natural gas supply.
- Fossil fuel power generation onsite produces **scope 1 emissions** (direct carbon emissions from onsite fuel combustion), adding substantially to AI’s carbon footprint.
- Additional considerations:
  - **Scope 2 emissions** derive from the carbon footprint of grid electricity purchased.
  - **Scope 3 emissions** include embedded carbon from materials, construction, and supply chains.
- Data centers contribute roughly 0.5% of US greenhouse gas emissions currently, with forecasts predicting 44 megatons by 2030—akin to adding 10 million gas-powered cars.
- While some centers use renewable energy, the industry broadly prioritizes rapid deployment over sustainability, citing time constraints contradictory to the long lead times required for fossil fuel plant construction.

This strategy reflects a tension between urgency in AI development and environmental stewardship, revealing much about corporate priorities.

## Societal and Community Consequences

AI data centers are not abstract infrastructures; they impact real communities in tangible ways, particularly in historically marginalized neighborhoods.

- The example of **xAI's Colossus data center** shows how excessive use of gas turbines (up to 35 operating despite permits for 15) has increased local smog by 30–60%, contributing to pollution linked to respiratory and cardiovascular diseases.
- Pollution estimates attribute about 1,300 premature deaths annually in affected areas to emissions from data centers.
- Community engagement and transparency are generally lacking:
  - Developers often operate through **shell companies**, obscure negotiations, and **non-disclosure agreements**, leaving residents uninformed until projects are far advanced.
  - Examples include Google and Meta data center developments shrouded in secrecy, with little local input allowed.
- Economically, data centers offer strong financial incentives for municipalities via **tax revenues**, sometimes reaching a billion dollars in hubs like Loudon County, Virginia.
- Conversely, most states offer **tax incentives and breaks** that undermine public benefits by transferring wealth to corporations.
- Claims that data centers provide substantial **local employment** are misleading; most centers need few technicians and security staff, challenging common assumptions about job creation.
- Communities face a complex calculus weighing immediate financial benefits against long-term environmental, health, and social costs—often with little access to reliable information for informed decisions.

## The Broader Context: Efficiency, Climate, and Future Choices

Two critical themes of this chapter emerge when synthesizing the data and discourse:

1. **Technological efficiency does not guarantee sustainability**: The **Jevons Paradox** highlights that efficiency gains in AI compute often lead to increased overall consumption as costs and usage expand.
2. **The environmental impact of AI is local and global**: Power and water usage affect local communities’ infrastructure and health, while greenhouse emissions contribute to global climate change—an issue transcending regional boundaries and requiring collective responsibility.

Proponents argue AI can also be a tool for **combatting climate change** by enabling efficiencies in other sectors, potentially reducing global emissions by 10% by 2030. However, these benefits are speculative and not yet concretely balanced against current and projected environmental costs.

The discussion underscores the pressing need for **transparent data, rigorous measurement**, and **inclusive policymaking** to weigh AI’s costs and benefits fairly. Without accountability and full costing—including environmental externalities—market forces incentivize suboptimal outcomes.

## Conclusion

The rise of hyperscale AI data centers embodies a complex trade-off between technological advancement and environmental sustainability. Although AI’s promise is substantial, the **full costs of development**—encompassing energy and water consumption, carbon emissions, local pollution, community disruption, and opaque governance—are only partially understood and rarely transparently shared.

Key conclusions include:

- AI data centers already consume significant shares of US electricity and water, with rapid growth expected amid ongoing uncertainty and secrecy.
- Behind-the-meter fossil fuel power plants are becoming a prevalent means to sustain data center growth, posing considerable challenges for climate goals.
- Local communities often bear disproportionate environmental and social harms, despite receiving tax revenues and minimaljob creation benefits.
- The **Jevons Paradox** warns that gains in efficiency are unlikely to reduce AI’s overall impact without deliberate policies guiding growth.
- Achieving a sustainable AI future requires clear measurement, balanced policy decisions, and a societal consensus on acceptable trade-offs.

Ultimately, **human intelligence must govern the trajectory of AI technology**, ensuring that decisions reflect accurate data, community voices, and equitable consideration of environmental costs. As this technology solidifies its place in society, asking not just *can* we build AI this way, but *should* we, becomes an imperative question.

### Summary of Key Points

- Hyperscale AI data centers are extraordinary consumers of electricity and water, with growing footprints difficult to precisely quantify due to secrecy and industry opacity.
- Energy efficiency improvements historically slowed power growth in computing but may be overwhelmed by AI’s demands (Koomey’s Law vs. the AI shift).
- Water use for cooling is a serious and local constraint; water scarcity exacerbates tensions in places like Arizona.
- Data centers increasingly bypass grid limitations by building fossil fuel power plants onsite, increasing carbon emissions (scope 1, 2, 3).
- Local pollution from data centers causes significant health consequences in adjacent communities.
- Economic benefits for local governments derive mostly from tax revenues, often facilitated by contentious tax breaks.
- Job creation at data centers is limited and does not compensate for the environmental and social costs borne by host communities.
- The Jevons Paradox applies: increased efficiency can paradoxically increase total resource consumption.
- The climate benefits of AI are speculative; current environmental costs are concrete and growing.
- Transparency, community involvement, and regulatory oversight are urgently needed to balance AI’s risks and rewards.

This chapter reveals the urgent but nuanced conversation society must have about AI’s environmental and social costs and challenges simplistic narratives ignoring these critical dimensions.
