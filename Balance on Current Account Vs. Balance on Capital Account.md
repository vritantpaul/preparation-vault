>Balance on current account and balance on capital account are interrelated.
>- A deficit in the current account must be settled by a surplus on the capital account. 
>- A surplus in the current account must be matched by a deficit on the capital account

Think of a country’s Balance of Payments (BoP) as a full money record with two main parts:

Current Account → tracks trade and income (exports, imports, remittances, services)

Capital Account → tracks money movement (investments, loans, foreign capital)



---

🔄 Why they are linked

The key idea:
👉 Every international transaction has two sides — money going out or coming in must be balanced somewhere.

So:

1. Current Account Deficit → Capital Account Surplus

If a country imports more than it exports, it has a deficit.

Example:

India imports goods worth $100

Exports only $70
➡️ Deficit = $30


How does India pay that extra $30?

👉 By bringing money from abroad:

Foreign investment (FDI, FII)

Borrowing loans

Selling assets


➡️ This creates a surplus in the capital account


---

2. Current Account Surplus → Capital Account Deficit

If a country exports more than it imports, it earns extra money.

Example:

Exports = $100

Imports = $70
➡️ Surplus = $30


What happens to that extra $30?

👉 The country sends money abroad:

Invests in other countries

Lends money

Buys foreign assets


➡️ This leads to a deficit in the capital account


---

⚖️ The Big Rule (Super Important)

👉 Current Account + Capital Account = 0 (approximately)

This happens because:

Money leaving one account must appear in the other

It’s like a double-entry accounting system



---

🧠 Simple Analogy

Think of it like your personal finances:

You spend more than you earn → you borrow money (capital inflow)

You earn more than you spend → you invest money elsewhere (capital outflow)



---

📌 In One Line

👉 Current account shows trade imbalance; capital account shows how that imbalance is financed.
